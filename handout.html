Exercise & dementia

A practical, evidence-informed conversation starter for Alzheimer's disease, other dementia, and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

12RCTs reviewed
1,924participants
12–24weeks in most programs

What does the review say?

  • Physical performance: 9 studies reported improvement; the most consistent outcome.
  • Cognition: 6 improved, 3 no significant change, 3 mixed.
  • ADLs / quality of life: 6 improved, 1 no change; 5 did not report.

Bottom line: Exercise can be a reasonable way to support function. A specific cognitive benefit cannot be promised.

Exercise types to consider

  • Aerobic: walking, seated marching
  • Strength: sit-to-stand, supported heel raises
  • Balance: supported weight shifts, target stepping
  • Gait: short familiar routes and turns

Examples are educational illustrations, not tested standardized prescriptions.

Individualize every session

Confirm medical status and fall risk. Use a safe environment, appropriate support, short one-step cues, demonstration, rest breaks, and activities the person enjoys. Avoid unsafe or distressing activities; monitor responses.

Suggested program characteristics

The project review suggests supervised, individualized multimodal activities at a moderate intensity where appropriate, approximately 2–5 sessions/week over 12–24 weeks, adapted to capacity.

From knowledge to action

  1. Educate and obtain leadership support.
  2. Ask clinicians about implementation barriers.
  3. Integrate appropriate exercises and document care.
  4. Compare baseline and 12-week survey/EMR measures; re-audit 12 weeks later.
Source: KTA Project Part 1 — Exercise and Dementia (supplied project summary). Full citations and context: see the Resources page.
Educational only. Not an official UPMC resource, patient-specific prescription, or claim that exercise reverses dementia.
